Research

I develop methods and open-source tools that give provable guarantees for AI-enabled and autonomous systems, including when their models are learned from data or unknown. Each tab below introduces one topic and the papers behind it.

Neural network verification

How can we prove what a neural network will do for every input in a set, not only the inputs we tested?

Propagating sets, not points

Testing a neural network means feeding it one input at a time and checking each answer. Verification asks a stronger question: what can the network output for every input in a set? The set might be every version of an image within a small change in brightness, or every state a drone could be in at take-off. Instead of sampling from that set, we push the whole set through the network, layer by layer, and look at the set of outputs it can produce.

Each linear layer maps a set to another set exactly and cheaply. The difficulty is the nonlinear activation functions, such as ReLU, which bend and fold the set as it passes through.

Exact or over-approximate?

An exact method splits the set wherever an activation can switch on or off, so the output is a union of pieces that matches exactly what the network can produce. It is sound and complete: if it reports a violation, a real counterexample exists. The price is that the number of pieces can grow exponentially with the size of the network.

An over-approximate method keeps a single convex set at each layer that is guaranteed to contain every reachable output. It scales far better and is still sound: if the over-approximation avoids the unsafe outputs, so does the network. If the two overlap, the answer is “unknown”, and the set can be split to tighten it. Tools such as NNV and n2v represent these sets as star sets, which support both kinds of analysis [1, 2]. Each year the International Verification of Neural Networks Competition (VNN-COMP) compares verification tools on shared benchmarks [8].

What can be guaranteed

Once we have the output set, a property is a question about that set:

  • Robustness: every perturbed version of an image receives the same label.
  • Safety of a control loop: a neural network controller, run together with the system it controls, never drives the system out of its safe region. The output set of one step becomes the input set of the next [3].
  • New architectures: the same questions for graph neural networks [4] and mixture-of-experts models [5].

For very large models, or inputs such as video and 3D scans, even over-approximation can be too slow. Probabilistic verification then gives a guarantee that holds with a chosen high probability, computed from samples of the network’s behaviour; learning the shape of the reachable set makes these guarantees much tighter [6, 1]. The same guarantees carry across vision, speech and depth models [9], including speech recognition for air traffic control, where a misheard instruction is safety-critical [10]. The same idea of checking a model’s output against a formal description also lets us catch and correct mistakes in the reasoning of AI systems [7].

Safe autonomy and robotics

Bringing formal guarantees onto real robots: certified drone flight, verified robot decision-making, and safety under physical constraints.

Certified flight through a doorway

A control barrier function acts as a safety filter: it watches the commands sent to a robot and changes them only when needed to keep the robot inside a certified safe set. In a cluttered indoor space a single barrier function struggles. To cover two rooms joined by a narrow doorway it must be either so cautious that little of either room counts as safe, or so loose that its guarantee fails exactly where the space pinches.

Our approach instead covers the free space with a union of many small neural control barrier functions, each verified on its own, and certifies which handovers between them are safe. The handovers form a graph, and a mission is a path through it. Each check is exact: it either proves a piece safe or returns a concrete state where it fails, never an inconclusive answer. The same analysis says, before any training, whether a passage is simply too narrow for any such union to carry the drone through it [1].

On the drone, safety is enforced at run time in layers. A tracker steers towards the next waypoint; a supervisor keeps track of which safe region the drone is in and when it may hand over to the next; and the filter adjusts each command so the drone stays inside the active region. If no region can safely be held or entered, the supervisor stops the drone rather than guessing, and it ends the mission only if that stop lasts more than ten seconds. The drone’s position comes from visual markers around the lab, fused with its own motion sensors, and the estimate grows less certain the longer the drone goes without seeing a marker.

Across flights through the doorway and around a structural column, no logged state left the certified set while the filter was running. With the filter removed, the drone left the set and was logged outside it at more than a hundred locations. Staying inside the safe set is something the filter guarantees, not something the unfiltered controller sometimes manages [1].

Tracking a moving target

The same lab is also where we test drones on targets that move. In this flight a small consumer drone follows a remote-controlled truck around the lab with nobody on the sticks: a detector finds the truck in the drone’s camera feed, and a computer flies the drone over Wi-Fi to keep the truck in view, backing off when the truck comes too close.

Adversarial attacks on drone following

Detectors like this are neural networks, and a small, deliberate change to their input can fool them. In the flight below, a drone uses its detector to find a small orange car in its camera images and follows the car from about two metres back. Partway through, we attack the detector: each camera image is changed slightly, with every pixel moved by at most 16/255 of its range in the direction that most increases the network’s error (an FGSM attack). The changed images look almost the same, but the detector no longer finds the car. The drone loses it and starts searching, first turning on the spot and then flying across the room. When the attack is switched off the car is already out of view, and the drone only finds it again 17 seconds later.

Robustness verification asks whether any change of that size could alter the detector’s answer for a given image. When the proof succeeds, no attack within that bound can work on that image, however it is crafted.

Verifying behaviour trees

Behaviour trees are a popular way to program what a robot does. Small actions and conditions are arranged in a tree, and sequence and fallback nodes decide which one runs next. Trees are easy to build and reuse, but mistakes can hide in how the pieces interact, for example a fallback that is never reached or two branches that fight over the same actuator.

BehaVerify translates a behaviour tree into a model that a model checker can explore exhaustively, so properties such as “the robot always returns to base when the battery is low” are proved for every possible run. Decisions made by neural networks can be included, and the same specification generates the code that runs on the robot, so the verified model and the deployed behaviour cannot drift apart [2]. We are now extending this to teams of robots whose trees must be composed safely [3].

Safety under physical constraints

Robot arms and many physical systems are described by equations with algebraic constraints, such as links that must stay connected. I helped develop the first method to synthesise control barrier functions for these systems, demonstrated on a three-link manipulator [4]. A companion paper covers semi-explicit differential-algebraic systems, with the barrier functions checked by sum-of-squares verification [5].

Data-driven control

Safety and stability guarantees when the model of the system is unknown and all we have is data.

Classical verification starts from a mathematical model of the system. Real systems rarely come with an accurate one, but we can usually observe them. I work on two different routes from data to a formal guarantee, and they give different kinds of answer.

Route 1: the scenario approach

Treat each observation, such as a recorded trajectory or a measured disturbance, as a scenario, and write it as a constraint. Then solve a convex optimisation problem that satisfies every scenario. Scenario theory, developed by Campi and Garatti, bounds the chance that the solution is violated by a new, unseen scenario: with confidence at least 1−β, the probability of violation is at most ε. The bound depends only on the number of samples and on how many of them shaped the solution. It needs no assumption about the probability distribution the data came from.

I have used this to estimate how fast the trajectories of an unknown system can spread apart, which is enough to build a finite abstraction and synthesise a controller for temporal logic specifications [1].

Scen-Opt makes the scenario approach available as a toolbox and web app for linear, quadratic and semidefinite programs [2].

Route 2: Willems’ fundamental lemma

For linear systems, and some classes of polynomial ones, a single trajectory can contain everything there is to know about the system, provided the input was varied enough. This condition is called persistence of excitation and is checked with a rank test on the recorded data. When it holds, every other trajectory of the system is a combination of the recorded one, so the unknown model can be replaced by matrices of data.

The data matrices can then be placed directly into a sum-of-squares program that searches for a control Lyapunov function (for stability) or a control barrier certificate (for safety), together with the controller that enforces it. Unlike the scenario approach, the resulting guarantee is deterministic. TRUST automates this for four classes of systems from one input-state trajectory [3]. I have extended the idea to neural network barrier certificates verified with SMT solvers [4], and to systems with algebraic constraints, where the data alone is not rich enough until it is collected in the right subspace; this enables a user to hand control safely to an autonomous controller once enough data has been gathered [5].

Stochastic systems

Two ways to prove what a noisy system will do: build a finite abstraction, or find a barrier certificate.

When a system is affected by noise, such as wind on a drone or demand on a power grid, no controller can promise that every run is safe. The right question becomes a probability: what is the probability that the system stays safe, or reaches its target while avoiding danger? I lead two tools that answer this question in different ways.

Abstraction: IMPaCT

The first approach divides the continuous state space into a finite grid of cells. For every cell and control input, we compute an interval that contains the probability of moving to each other cell. The result is an interval Markov decision process, a finite model that is guaranteed to contain the behaviour of the real system. An algorithm called interval iteration then computes lower and upper bounds on the probability of satisfying the specification, together with a controller that achieves the lower bound.

Abstraction handles rich specifications such as safety, reachability and reach-while-avoid, but the number of cells grows exponentially with the dimension of the system. IMPaCT tackles this by building the abstraction in parallel on CPUs and GPUs [1]. Abstractions can also be learned from data, with a quantified error, using Gaussian processes [2].

Barrier certificates: PRoTECT

The second approach avoids the grid altogether. A barrier certificate is a function over the state space that is low on the initial states, high on the unsafe states, and does not grow too quickly in expectation as the system evolves. Finding such a function proves an upper bound on the probability of ever reaching the unsafe states within a time horizon, without simulating a single run. For polynomial systems, the search for a barrier certificate becomes a sum-of-squares optimisation problem.

PRoTECT searches over several polynomial degrees in parallel and covers discrete- and continuous-time systems, both stochastic and deterministic [3]. Barrier certificates scale better with dimension than a grid, at the cost of more conservative probability bounds and a focus on safety. Both tools take part in the stochastic category of the ARCH-COMP verification competition [4].

Model reduction and abstraction

Design controllers on a small model, with a proof of how closely the large system follows it.

Formal controller synthesis gives guarantees, but its cost grows quickly with the number of states. One way round this is to design the controller on a smaller model and then prove how far the large system can drift from it. A simulation function is a Lyapunov-like function over both systems that bounds the distance between their outputs, so a guarantee on the small model carries over to the large one with a known error.

Robust simulation functions

Real systems face large disturbances, and these usually make the error bound too loose to be useful. Robust simulation functions with disturbance refinement pass part of the disturbance to the reduced-order model through an interface function, which keeps the error small. With this, a controller for a 9-state area of the New England 39-bus power system was designed from a 3-state model [1]. The journal version makes the method compositional: assume-guarantee contracts give each subsystem its own controller, and the local guarantees combine into a safety guarantee for the whole network. It also covers nonlinear terms such as the sine functions in power-system models [2].

Projected systems with algebraic constraints

Many physical systems are written as differential-algebraic equations, where algebraic constraints keep the state on a lower-dimensional manifold. Power networks and robot arms with linked joints are two examples. Standard control barrier functions ignore these constraints and can produce safety filters that are impossible to apply. We project the dynamics onto the constraint manifold and build barrier functions that respect it, checked by sum-of-squares verification [3]. A companion paper turns the problem into barrier synthesis for an ordinary differential equation with extra compatibility constraints, then lifts the result back to the full state space [4].

My PhD thesis brings the simulation-function work together with its power-system case studies [5].

Applications

Where the methods are used: robotics, power, water, air traffic control, medical devices and agriculture.

The methods on the other tabs are tested on real systems. These are the domains where a paper is built around one application.

Drones and robotics

Drones, robot teams and other autonomous vehicles are the most direct application, and they have their own section: see Safe autonomy and robotics.

Power systems

Power grids must keep their frequency inside tight limits, even after a sudden loss of generation. I designed the first formally verified controller for frequency regulation of a power grid, using a population of electric vehicles to support the Great Britain grid after a large outage [1]. Model reduction makes larger grids possible: controllers for the New England 39-bus system are designed on reduced-order models with a guaranteed error, first for one area and then for the whole interconnected network [2, 3].

Graph neural networks are now used as fast stand-ins for power flow, optimal power flow and cascading failure analysis. With Anne Tumlin, we extended NNV to verify these networks under changes to both bus and line features, on the IEEE 24-, 39- and 118-bus test cases [4]. This paper also appears under neural network verification.

Buildings are turning from passive loads into active players that generate, store and share energy. Three book chapters cover how to control and manage active buildings, both one at a time and as a population at network level, and how to model smart homes as cyber-physical systems [5, 6, 7]. My PhD thesis collects the power-system work [8].

Water systems

Water resource recovery facilities treat wastewater and must meet legal limits even during extreme rainfall. With Anna Laino and colleagues, we proposed a resilience metric based on signal temporal logic. It finds the largest stress a facility can take while still meeting its limits on biochemical and chemical oxygen demand, and it works on data that water companies already collect [9]. The paper was chosen for the journal's 2024 highlights collection.

Air traffic control

Pilots and controllers talk over a noisy VHF radio channel, and speech-to-text models are starting to transcribe these clearances. For each individual message, we certify how likely radio noise is to change what the model decodes, with a distribution-free bound from repeated noise samples. We produced over 450,000 such certificates for four public speech-to-text systems on two public air traffic control datasets [10]. This paper also appears under neural network verification.

Medical devices

Implanted medical devices must not fail. CANDO is an optogenetic brain implant that aims to prevent epileptic seizures. My MComp dissertation built a formal model of its controller from the source code and found design errors such as unreachable states and wrong transitions [11]. A later paper proved the command interface of its chip correct in the Isabelle/HOL theorem prover [12].

Agriculture

Robotti is a semi-autonomous agricultural robot. We used data from its field tests to tune the parameters of a co-simulation model of the robot, so that the model matches the real machine more closely [13].
